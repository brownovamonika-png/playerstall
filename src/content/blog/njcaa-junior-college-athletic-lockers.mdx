## NJCAA Junior College Athletic Lockers: A Buyer's Guide

Junior college athletic departments operate under a set of constraints that no
four-year program shares, and almost none of the locker room advice written for
colleges accounts for them.

The roster turns over completely every two years. The budget is a fraction of a
Division I department's. The athletes are being actively recruited by schools
with more money. And in many programs, athletics is a meaningful driver of
enrolment, which means the facilities are doing admissions work as well as
athletic work.

That combination changes what a good locker room decision looks like.

## The recruiting argument, stated honestly

A junior college is usually recruiting one of two athletes: one who wants to
play immediately rather than redshirt at a four-year school, or one who intends
to use two years to earn a transfer.

Both are comparing your program against others. And on a campus visit, the
locker room is one of very few things they can actually judge.

They cannot assess your coaching from a tour. They cannot evaluate your
strength program in twenty minutes. But they can see whether the room they
would dress in every day looks like somewhere a program invested, or somewhere
a program gave up.

This is not a claim that lockers win recruits. It is a narrower and more
defensible point: **the locker room is one of the few signals a recruit can
read directly, and it is read whether or not you intend it to be.** A room with
dented metal doors that no longer close communicates something specific about
how the athlete will be treated, and no amount of conversation on the tour
undoes it.

For a department where athletics supports enrolment, that signal is doing more
than athletic work.

## Two-year turnover changes the arithmetic

Here is the thing junior college departments consistently underweight.

Your athletes are there for two years. Your lockers are there for fifteen.

A locker bought this year will be used by roughly ten separate cohorts of
athletes, and seen by every recruiting class in between. The purchase is not
being made for the current roster, it is being made for every roster for the
next decade and a half.

That reframes the cheap-versus-durable decision. A bank that looks tired after
four years has disappointed two cohorts and will disappoint six more before
anyone finds the budget to replace it. The replacement cost, when it comes,
competes against that year's recruiting budget.

This is the argument for buying once, properly, in a material that ages well.
Sealed wood does not dent, does not rust where a coating chips, and can be
refinished rather than replaced. We guarantee ours for five years, and the
useful life is considerably longer than that.

## Sharing rooms across sports and seasons

Most junior college departments cannot give every sport a dedicated room, and
should not try.

Sharing works when it is organised **by season rather than by squeezing**:

- **Autumn**: football, soccer, volleyball, cross country
- **Winter**: basketball, wrestling, indoor track
- **Spring**: baseball, softball, track, golf, tennis

A room assigned to the autumn sport from August to November and the winter sport
from November to March serves both properly. A room where a football player and
a basketball player are permanently assigned the same locker serves neither.

What makes seasonal sharing work in practice:

- **Removable name plates** at $10 each, swapped at handover rather than
  engraved permanently
- **A locker wide enough for the bulkiest sport using it**, if football shares
  with basketball, specify for football
- **A handover date agreed between coaching staff before the season**, not
  negotiated during it
- **A keypad lock box** rather than issued keys, so there is nothing to collect
  back from a departing roster

## Phasing across budget years

Very few junior college departments can outfit every sport in one purchase, and
there is no reason to pretend otherwise.

Phasing works. What determines whether it works *well* is consistency.

**Specify the model and finish once, at the start.** Then every phase matches
the last. Three rooms bought across three budget years in the same model read
as a department executing a plan. Three rooms in three different models read as
three separate scrambles.

**Order by room, not by partial room.** Finishing one space completely is worth
more than half-finishing two.

**Start where visits happen.** If recruiting tours go through the basketball
room, that is the first room.

Our minimum order is 8 lockers, which is small enough to phase a sport at a
time without penalty. And because our prices are published rather than
negotiated, the number you budget in year one is the number that is still true
in year three.

## Specification notes for junior college rooms

A few things that come up more in two-year programs than elsewhere.

**Commuter athletes need more secure storage.** A significant share of junior
college athletes live off campus, work around training, and carry their whole
day with them, laptop, wallet, keys, work clothes. A keypad lock box at $80
earns its place more here than in a residential four-year program.

**No key management.** A roster that turns over completely every two years makes
issued keys an administrative problem. Keypad codes that reset do not.

**Logos matter for identity.** At $75 per locker, a program mark is one of the
cheapest things in the build and one of the most visible on a tour. For a
department building identity on a limited budget, it is good value.

**Ventilation, because rooms get shared.** A room used by two or three sports
across a year has gear in it more months than a single-sport room. Vented fronts
and a raised base matter correspondingly more.

## Conclusion

Junior college athletic departments are asked to recruit against better-funded
schools, with rosters that empty every two years, on budgets that rarely allow a
whole department to be done at once. None of that is going to change.

What is within reach is a locker room decision that works with those
constraints instead of against them: buy for the fifteen years the furniture
will be there rather than the two years the current roster will, phase by room
in a consistent model so the department accumulates something coherent, share by
season rather than by squeezing, and spend the small money, logos, name plates,
lock boxes, where athletes and recruits actually notice it.
