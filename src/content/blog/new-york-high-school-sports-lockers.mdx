## New York High School Sports Lockers: A Guide for Athletic Directors

New York runs one of the largest high school athletic systems in the country,
spread across eleven NYSPHSAA sections and an enormous range of school types, 
rural districts in the North Country, suburban programs on Long Island and in
Westchester, and the dense urban schools of New York City.

An athletic director in Plattsburgh and one in Queens are solving genuinely
different locker room problems. This guide covers both, plus the parts that are
the same everywhere in the state.

## What is actually different about New York

Three things come up in nearly every conversation we have with a New York
program.

### Winter is a design input

Upstate New York winters put more gear on athletes than almost anywhere else we
sell. From November through March, a student arrives with a parka, boots, hat
and gloves on top of whatever the sport requires. That is a genuine volume
problem, a locker sized comfortably for a soccer kit in September is full
before the gear goes in come January.

Then there is **road salt**. It comes in on every boot, dissolves in meltwater,
and settles wherever the water goes. Salt is aggressively corrosive to metal. On
a powder-coated steel locker it finds every chip and scratch, and rust spreads
outward from there, which is why so many upstate schools have metal banks with
rusted bottoms and intact tops.

The specification answers are straightforward:

- **Wider lockers** than you would specify in a warmer state. Twenty-four inches
  minimum, and 28 or 30 where budget allows.
- **A raised base**, so meltwater and salt do not sit against the bottom edge.
- **A sealed finish** that can be wiped down through the winter.
- **Hooks**, so outerwear hangs rather than filling the shelf.

### The buildings are old

New York has a lot of school buildings that have been in service for fifty years
or more, and athletic spaces in them are rarely regular rectangles. Columns in
the middle of rooms, low ceilings, sloped floors, and alcoves that a standard
locker bank cannot use.

This is where custom widths genuinely matter. In our line a non-standard width
costs the same per locker as a standard one, so building to the actual room
rather than to a catalogue size usually yields **more usable positions**, not
fewer. An alcove that fits nothing standard will often take three lockers at a
width chosen for it.

Measure before you shortlist. We would rather quote against real dimensions than
against an assumed layout.

### The budget process is its own constraint

New York districts generally work on a 1 July budget year, with capital projects
and athletic equipment moving on different tracks and different approval
timelines.

Two practical consequences:

**Order in spring, install in summer.** Custom builds run 10 to 12 weeks.
Summer is the only period when a locker room can be worked on without
displacing teams. An order placed in May or early June installs during the
break. One placed in August is arriving mid-season.

**Published prices help with approval.** A figure you can put in a budget
request in February and still rely on in June is worth more to an athletic
director than a discount negotiated under pressure. Ours are published, which is
mostly why.

## Upstate and downstate, specifically

**Upstate and Western New York.** Winter gear volume is the dominant factor.
Programs here should size up rather than down, specify for salt and meltwater,
and plan for athletes arriving in full outdoor kit for several months of the
year. Sections 1 through 7 cover enormous geographic distances, which also
means long bus trips and athletes carrying more with them.

**Long Island and Westchester.** Larger athletic budgets and strong competitive
programs, with locker rooms frequently functioning as a recruiting and retention
signal within the district. More demand for finishes and logos. Space is less
often the constraint; expectations are higher.

**New York City.** Space is the constraint, nearly always. Dense buildings,
shared facilities, multiple programs through the same room in a day. The
priorities shift to turnover, durability under heavy use, and fitting the
maximum usable positions into a fixed footprint. Keypad lock boxes do a lot of
work here, since a room used by several groups cannot rely on assigned keys.

## Sharing rooms, which nearly every New York school does

Very few New York high schools have a dedicated room per sport. Most share by
season, and the thing that determines whether it works is the handover.

- **Autumn**: football, soccer, volleyball, cross country, field hockey
- **Winter**: basketball, wrestling, indoor track, swimming
- **Spring**: baseball, softball, lacrosse, track, tennis

Specify the room for the bulkiest sport that uses it, use removable name plates
at $10 rather than permanent assignment, and agree handover dates between
coaches before the season starts.

Where a room genuinely serves two sports at once, which happens most often in
spring, specify wider lockers than either sport alone would need and accept
that as the cost of the shared space.

## A note on metal

Most New York schools we talk to are replacing metal, and the reasons are
consistent: dented doors that no longer close, rusted bottoms where salt and
meltwater sat, and noise.

Metal makes sense in some settings. A New York locker room that takes road salt
five months a year is not one of them. Sealed wood does not rust where a
coating chips, does not dent from a bag or a boot, and can be refinished rather
than replaced.

## Conclusion

New York athletic directors are specifying locker rooms for a longer, harder
winter than most of the country, frequently inside buildings that were finished
before the sport in question had a girls' team, on a budget calendar that gives
one realistic installation window a year.

The decisions that matter are not complicated. Size up for winter outerwear.
Specify a raised base and a finish that takes salt and meltwater. Measure the
real room rather than assuming a standard bank will fit, because custom widths
cost no more and usually gain you positions. Order in spring so the work happens
in summer. And if you are phasing across budget years, lock the model and finish
in the first order so the rooms match as they accumulate.
