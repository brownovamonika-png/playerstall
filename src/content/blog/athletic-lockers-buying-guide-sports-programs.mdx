**Most athletic directors make their locker purchase decision too fast and on too little information. The supplier quotes a per-unit price. The AD compares it to the last price they remember. The purchase goes through. Five years later, the lockers are showing real wear and the program is back to planning a replacement cycle that shouldn't have been necessary. This guide is built to break that pattern.**

## Why Material is the Most Important Decision

If you only retain one thing from this guide, make it this: the material your lockers are built from determines everything else. Performance, longevity, maintenance burden, customisation capability, and total cost over 20 years all flow from material choice. Every other specification is secondary.

There are three real options in the athletic locker market: solid wood, metal, and particleboard/laminate. They are not comparable alternatives. They perform at completely different levels over a 15–20 year facility lifespan.

### Solid Wood: The Performance Standard

Solid 3/4" birch — the construction we've used for over 30 years — is dense enough to resist denting and surface damage from daily athletic use. It doesn't corrode in the humid, post-practice environment of a locker room. It absorbs sound rather than amplifying it, which matters more than you'd think in a room full of athletes using lockers simultaneously. Most importantly, it accepts custom finishes, laser engraving, and full-color branding in ways metal and laminate simply can't.

The birch we use isn't the 1/2" material some manufacturers call "wood construction." It's genuine 3/4" structural birch — the same thickness a cabinet maker would use for furniture expected to last generations. When we say lifetime guarantee, we mean it because we know exactly what we built.

### Metal: The Familiarity Trap

Metal lockers are what most programs have because they're what programs have always had. They're familiar, they look institutional, and they're relatively inexpensive upfront. They're also the worst-performing option by every metric that matters over a real facility lifecycle.

Metal lockers in active athletic use show meaningful degradation within 5–7 years: dents from equipment contact, peeling paint in areas of repeated friction, rust at corners and hinges in humid environments, and loose hardware from repeated door impact. By year 10–12, a metal locker room looks visibly worn and runs daily frustrations for athletes and coaching staff. The 8–12 year effective lifespan of metal locker installations means your $200-per-unit "savings" over wood becomes a second full replacement cycle — plus installation labor — within the lifespan of a single wood installation.

### Particleboard/Laminate: The Most Expensive Cheap Option

Laminate lockers occupy the dangerous middle ground — they look appealing in a manufacturer's catalog, cost less than metal per unit, and fail faster than either alternative. The core material is particleboard, which is extremely susceptible to moisture. Athletic locker rooms generate continuous moisture from wet gear, post-shower humidity, and seasonal climate variation. Particleboard absorbs that moisture and swells. Seams separate. The laminate surface, designed for dry office environments, chips and peels at the corners, doors, and handle contact points that experience the most friction.

We've visited facilities where a 7-year-old laminate locker room looked worse than a 20-year-old metal installation. It's the most vivid illustration of sticker-price thinking versus lifecycle thinking you'll ever see. ## Sizing Guide by Sport

Locker dimensions are a function of equipment volume. Get the sizing wrong and athletes will improvise — gear ends up on floors, benches, and adjacent stations, and the locker room looks perpetually disorganized regardless of how much you paid for it.

Here are the configurations we've refined over 30 years of sport-specific builds.

### Football

30"–36" wide, 76"–84" tall. Football equipment is the most voluminous in team sports: shoulder pads, helmet, full uniform sets, practice gear, and typically multiple pairs of cleats. A 30" locker with a dedicated helmet shelf, shoulder pad hooks rated for 15+ lbs, and a ventilated base cubby for cleats is the minimum for a comfortable football locker room. Programs building flagship football facilities typically go to 36" with integrated equipment hooks at multiple heights.

### Hockey

30"–36" wide, 76" tall, with specialized interior configurations for skate storage. Hockey gear is bulky and wet after every use — skates, chest protectors, shoulder pads, and gloves must air out or they produce serious odor and degrade rapidly. Ventilated door panels and base cubbies with drainage angles are not optional in hockey locker rooms. See our detailed treatment in [our hockey lockers guide](/blog/hockey-lockers-guide) for full configuration specs.

### Basketball and Soccer

24"–27" wide, 72"–76" tall. These sports have lower equipment volume but athletes spend more time in the facility, which means charging integration and personal storage quality matter more proportionally. Shoe storage with airflow at the base, a mid-height shelf for accessories, and a full hanging bay for uniform sets covers the typical basketball or soccer athlete's daily needs.

### Baseball and Softball

24"–30" wide, 76" tall, with a dedicated vertical bat channel 48"–54" tall and 4"–6" wide. This is the configuration requirement that separates a genuine baseball locker from a generic configuration that happened to be assigned to a baseball team. Without proper bat storage, bats end up leaning against walls, getting knocked over, and creating floor hazards. Add a batting helmet shelf at mid-height and a ventilated base cubby for cleats. For full details, our [baseball wood lockers guide](/blog/baseball-wood-lockers-complete-guide) covers every configuration variable.

### Wrestling and Track

18"–24" wide, 72"–76" tall. These sports carry lighter per-athlete equipment loads — singlets, shoes, and limited personal gear. The storage priority is vertical adjustability: wrestling gear changes by training phase, and track athletes rotate equipment across multiple disciplines. Adjustable shelving beats fixed configurations for these sports.

## Customisation That Actually Matters

Every locker supplier will mention customisation. Not all customisation is equal. Some features drive real recruiting and athlete experience value. Others are impressive in a sales deck and largely irrelevant once lockers are in daily use.

### High-Impact Customisation

Player nameplates and jersey numbers are the single highest-value customisation per dollar. A recruit walks into your locker room on an official visit and sees their name above a locker. That moment is more powerful than anything in your facilities video. Nameplates can be swapped out between recruiting classes — budget for that.

Team color schemes and logo integration transform a locker room from a storage room into a genuine program environment. The visual identity of your program should be present in the locker room as strongly as it is on the field. Custom paint finishes, panel inlays in team colors, and hardware that matches your program's aesthetic palette add brand consistency that coaches, athletes, and recruits all notice.

Integrated charging stations — USB-A and USB-C ports built into each locker station — have moved from premium upgrade to expected feature at the high school varsity and collegiate level. Athletes under 22 live on their phones for film, communication, and music. A locker without a charger is a locker that gets partially ignored.

### Lower-Impact Customisation

Decorative panel carvings, external LED accent lighting, and smart lock systems are upgrades that look impressive in manufacturer renderings but have limited impact on the daily experience of your athletes. If your budget allows them after covering the high-impact items, fine. If you're choosing between nameplates and decorative carving, choose nameplates every time.

## How to Evaluate Vendors: 10 Questions

The athletic locker market has a wide range of suppliers — from manufacturers building genuine lifetime-quality products to resellers moving containerized offshore inventory with minimal quality control. These 10 questions separate them.

The questions every AD should ask any locker supplier are: What is the core material and nominal thickness of your panels? What does your warranty cover, and what does it specifically exclude? Do you offer a free design consultation before we're required to commit? Can you provide three references from programs of similar size and sport mix? Where is the product manufactured and what quality certifications apply? What customisation options are included in the base price versus charged as upgrades? What is your lead time from finalized design to delivery? Who handles installation and is that included in your quote? What happens if a locker arrives damaged — what is the replacement process and timeline? How do you handle warranty claims after installation is complete?

A credible supplier answers every one of these directly and specifically. Vague answers to warranty coverage, deflection on manufacturing location, or inability to provide comparable references are meaningful signals about what your post-purchase experience will look like.

## Budget Planning and Total Cost of Ownership

Per-unit cost is the most misleading number in an athletic locker purchase. It hides the variable that actually determines value: how long the locker lasts before requiring replacement, and what that replacement costs.

### The 20-Year TCO Model

Consider a 30-unit athletic locker room. Our Varsity tier at $449 per unit = $13,470 upfront, with a lifetime guarantee covering the full installation period. No replacement cycle.

A metal locker at a typical market price of $220–$260 per unit = $6,600–$7,800 upfront, with a realistic replacement cycle at year 10–12 of another $6,600–$7,800 plus $3,000–$5,000 in installation labor for the second cycle. Twenty-year total: $16,200–$20,600 — and that assumes only one replacement cycle.

Particleboard/laminate at $150–$200 per unit = $4,500–$6,000 upfront, with a likely replacement cycle every 7–8 years. Over 20 years, that's potentially three purchase-and-install cycles: $13,500–$18,000 in product plus $6,000–$10,000 in labor. Twenty-year total: $19,500–$28,000.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Several patterns in the athletic locker market consistently signal poor post-purchase outcomes.

Vague warranty language is the biggest red flag. A warranty that excludes "normal wear," "environmental conditions," or "commercial use" in an athletic setting is functionally worthless — those exclusions cover almost every failure mode a locker will experience in real use. Read the warranty before you sign any contract, not after.

No design consultation offering means a supplier is selling a product, not a solution. A legitimate locker manufacturer that has worked with athletic programs for any length of time knows that every room is different — different dimensions, different sports, different roster sizes, different compliance requirements. A supplier who hands you a catalog and asks you to pick a SKU is not equipped to serve your program's actual needs.

Offshore manufacturing with no quality transparency is increasingly common as demand for lower price points has pushed some suppliers to containerized imports. The result is products built to different material standards than what's described, with no accountability when those differences show up in year three.

Pressure to commit before a design is complete is a sales tactic. Any supplier who tells you pricing is only valid for 48 hours or that your spot in the production queue depends on a deposit today before you've seen a design proposal is using urgency as a substitute for quality.

## 5 Questions Every AD Should Answer Before Ordering

Before you contact any supplier, be able to answer these five questions clearly. Your answers will shape your specification and protect you in vendor conversations.

First: What sport or sports will use this locker room, and what is the maximum roster size for each? This determines your locker count and configuration requirements — don't guess on roster size, pull the actual number.

Second: What is your target installation date, and what is your absolute latest acceptable date? Summer installation windows are limited and fill up. Know your dates and work backward to set a consultation deadline.

Third: What is your total project budget — not just for lockers, but for the complete renovation including installation and ancillary work? If you don't know this number, you're not ready to talk to suppliers.

Fourth: What customisation elements are non-negotiable for your program? Player nameplates, specific team colors, charging stations — identify the must-haves before you get into a conversation where everything gets positioned as an upgrade.

Fifth: What does your procurement process require? Some school districts require a competitive bid process. Some allow sole-source purchases under certain thresholds. Knowing your procurement rules before you start saves weeks in the buying process.
