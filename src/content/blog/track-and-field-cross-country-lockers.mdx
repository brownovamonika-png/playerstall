## Track and Field and Cross Country Lockers: A Guide for Programs

Track and field is the biggest participation sport in American high schools, 
around 1.1 million athletes, according to [NFHS 2024-25 participation
figures](https://nfhs.org/stories/participation-in-high-school-sports-hits-record-high-with-sizable-increase-in-2024-25),
more than football. Cross country adds roughly another 440,000.

And yet track programs are routinely the worst-provisioned in the building.

The reason is arithmetic. A football team of 60 is a manageable locker order. A
track roster of 120 at the same per-locker price is double the cost, for a sport
with a smaller budget and less visibility. So track gets the leftovers: the old
bank nobody else wanted, or nothing at all.

That is a real constraint. But it is solvable, and the solution is not
"buy cheaper lockers for everyone."

## The roster problem, and how to actually solve it

The instinct is to divide the budget by the roster and buy whatever that
affords. That produces a room of undersized lockers that serves nobody well.

The better approach is to recognise that **a track roster is not one group of
athletes, it is four or five groups with completely different storage needs.**

**Sprinters and mid-distance.** The lightest kit in the program. Spikes, a
change of clothes, a warm-up layer, a bag. An 18 or 20-inch locker is genuinely
sufficient, not a compromise.

**Distance and cross country.** Similar volume, more mud, and more layers
because they train outdoors in worse weather for longer. Same width, more
attention to drying and dirt.

**Throwers.** Bulkier shoes, often a larger athlete with a larger bag, and
sometimes personal implements. These athletes need the wider positions.

**Vaulters and jumpers.** Their defining equipment does not belong in a locker
under any specification. A vaulting pole is 12 to 17 feet long.

**Multi-event athletes.** Carry more of everything and are usually there the
most. If you have a few, give them the best positions.

So the room becomes a tier rather than a uniform bank:

- A run of standard 18 to 20-inch lockers for the bulk of the roster
- A smaller run of 24-inch positions for throwers and multi-event athletes
- A secured team storage area for implements
- Shared or half-height positions for athletes who are there seasonally

That costs considerably less than 120 identical lockers and serves the program
considerably better.

## Spikes, mud and the base shelf

Every track and cross country storage problem eventually comes back to what is
on the athletes' feet.

**Spikes are abrasive and dirty.** Pins mark surfaces. Track grit and infield
dirt comes in on every shoe. Cross country adds genuine mud, grass and in late
season, ice.

**Spikes are also wet.** A cross country meet in November produces shoes that
will still be damp on Monday if they are stored in a sealed box.

The fix is the same one that works for softball and for rink dressing rooms,
and it is a specification decision rather than a budget one:

- **A vented or slatted base shelf**, so spikes sit above a surface air can move
  through, and the grit falls rather than packing.
- **Base clearance under the locker**, so dirt does not compact underneath where
  nobody can sweep.
- **A sealed finish** that takes repeated damp wiping without absorbing it.
- **Separation between the shoe shelf and everything else**, a clean warm-up
  layer should not be sitting on the surface the spikes came off onto.

## Implement storage is a separate project

This is the part track programs most often get wrong, and it is worth stating
plainly: **implements do not go in lockers.**

- **Vaulting poles** are 12 to 17 feet and need a horizontal rack with several
  support points, in a secured room. A pole stored badly takes a set and
  becomes unsafe.
- **Hurdles** stack, but they need floor space and a wall they can lean against,
  not a locker.
- **Shot, discus, javelin and hammer** need a secured cabinet. These are heavy,
  valuable and genuinely dangerous if they are accessible to anyone wandering
  through.
- **Starting blocks and batons** need a shelf somewhere findable.

If your budget conversation is "we cannot afford lockers for the whole roster,"
one useful answer is that part of what you thought was a locker problem is
actually a storage room problem, and a storage room is cheaper per square foot
than lockers are.

## Sharing with cross country, and sharing with everyone else

Most schools run track and cross country out of the same room, and most schools
also share that room with other sports across the year.

**Track and cross country together is easy.** Autumn and spring seasons, heavy
athlete overlap, near-identical storage needs. Specify for cross country, the
muddier of the two, and track is comfortably covered.

**Sharing with other sports is harder**, and the thing to watch is the calendar
overlap rather than the gear. Spring track overlaps with baseball, softball,
lacrosse and soccer in most states. If the same lockers are assigned to a track
athlete and a lacrosse player in April, you have not shared a room, you have
created a dispute.

Where sharing is unavoidable:

- Use **removable name plates** rather than permanent assignment, $10 each
- Specify **wider lockers** than either sport alone would need
- Agree the handover date between coaching staff before the season, not during

## Timing

Track has a scheduling advantage worth using. The season is spring, the budget
year usually turns in summer, and cross country runs in autumn.

That means an order placed after cross country finishes, November or December, 
lands comfortably before track opens. Programs that wait until February are
ordering into the season, and 10 to 12 weeks puts delivery somewhere around the
conference meet.

## Where track programs go wrong

**Sizing the whole room to the throwers.** The most expensive mistake available.
Tier instead.

**Trying to store implements in lockers.** Poles especially. It does not work,
and it puts equipment at risk.

**Buying one locker per athlete because that feels fair.** For a 120-athlete
roster it is usually unaffordable, and the result is 120 lockers too small to
be useful. A tiered room with real storage serves more athletes better.

**Specifying for the track and forgetting the cross country mud.** The autumn
sport is harder on the room than the spring one.

## Conclusion

Track and field has the largest roster and often the smallest locker budget in
the building. Those two facts are usually treated as an unsolvable squeeze, but
most of the squeeze comes from a specification choice, sizing every position
for the athlete who carries the most, and trying to store implements in
furniture that was never meant to hold them.

Tier the room. Size the sprinters honestly at 18 to 20 inches, give the throwers
and multi-event athletes the wider positions, put the poles and implements in a
secured storage area, and specify the base shelf and finish for cross country
mud rather than track grit. That room costs less and works better than 120
identical lockers.
