## Locker Room Renovation RFP: Template and Complete Guide for Athletic Directors

**Most athletic directors have never written a procurement RFP. That's not a criticism — it's a description of the job. You hire coaches, manage budgets, schedule facilities, and fight for your programs. Writing a formal Request for Proposals for a locker room renovation is a task that shows up once every 15–20 years, and there's almost no guidance specific to athletic locker rooms anywhere.**

**A poorly written RFP produces incomparable bids. Incomparable bids produce bad purchasing decisions. Bad purchasing decisions produce locker rooms that fail in 8 years when you needed 25. This guide gives you the template and the context to write an RFP that works.**

Public school athletic directors and those at state universities are typically required by purchasing rules to issue a formal RFP for capital purchases above a defined threshold — commonly $25,000–$50,000. Private institutions and independent colleges often have more flexibility, but a well-structured RFP protects your program even when it isn't required.

This guide covers when an RFP is required, what every section must include, how to write specifications that produce comparable bids, how to evaluate vendor responses, and what PlayerStall's proposal looks like so you know what to expect from a quality vendor.

## When an RFP Is Required

Public school districts in most states require competitive procurement for purchases exceeding a defined dollar threshold. Common thresholds:

Most state public education purchasing rules set the formal bid requirement at $25,000–$50,000 for capital expenditures. Some states require RFPs; others accept formal bid invitations (IFBs) for commodity purchases. Locker room renovations — which involve both goods (the lockers) and services (design and installation) — are typically classified as complex purchases that require the RFP format rather than a simple sealed bid.

Your school district's purchasing office or your institution's procurement department is the authoritative source on your specific threshold and process requirements. Check before you start. A purchase made without the required procurement process can be challenged, voided, or create audit findings.

### When You're Not Required But Should Anyway

Even when a formal RFP is not legally required, a structured evaluation process protects you. Athletic directors who make large facility purchases through informal conversations with a single vendor have no documentation of due diligence if the purchase is later questioned. A written RFP process — even a simplified one — creates a record of competitive evaluation that protects both the institution and the individual decision-maker.

## Why a Good RFP Saves You Money

The purpose of an RFP is not paperwork. It's forcing vendors to compete on a level playing field that gives you meaningful, comparable information.

Without a clear spec, here's what happens: Vendor A bids solid birch lockers at $449 each. Vendor B bids particleboard-core lockers with a birch veneer at $299 each. Without a specification distinguishing solid from composite construction, both bids appear to be "wood lockers." The evaluation committee picks Vendor B because the price is 33% lower. Five years later, the particleboard is delaminating in the high-humidity athletic environment and you're looking at a $40,000 replacement project.

The extra specificity in the RFP — "solid 3/4-inch birch, no particleboard, MDF, or laminate-over-substrate construction" — would have produced comparable bids for comparable products. It might have produced a higher price award. It would have saved $40,000 in year five.

This is not hypothetical. It is the story of dozens of programs we've encountered after losing a bid to a competitor who bid inferior materials. The programs call us when the replacement cycle comes. We wish we could have helped them the first time.

## The 8 Sections Every Locker Room RFP Needs

A complete locker room renovation RFP needs eight sections. Every section serves a specific function in producing usable, comparable bids.

### Section 1: Introduction and Scope of Work

This section describes the project: who is issuing the RFP, the facility location, the general scope of the work (number of lockers, approximate square footage, sport(s) served), and the overall project goals. It is not where you specify technical requirements — those go in Section 3. It is where you give vendors the context they need to determine whether to submit a proposal.

**Sample language:** "The [School/Institution Name] Athletic Department is soliciting proposals for the design, manufacture, delivery, and installation of custom athletic wood lockers for the [Sport] locker room at [Facility Name]. The project scope includes approximately [X] lockers in a [Y] square foot space. The target sport is [sport], with [roster size] athletes to be served."

### Section 2: Vendor Qualifications

Specify the minimum qualifications a vendor must demonstrate to be considered. This section prevents unqualified vendors from submitting proposals that waste everyone's evaluation time. Minimum qualifications for a locker room project should include:

Vendors must demonstrate at minimum five years of experience manufacturing custom athletic lockers. Vendors must provide three references from comparable programs (similar sport, similar program size, similar installation scope) completed within the last five years. Vendors must be able to provide proof of general liability insurance at the coverage level your institution requires.

### Section 3: Technical Specifications

This is the most important section. Vague specifications here create incomparable bids. Every dimension, material, hardware component, and finishing requirement should be specified explicitly.

Critical material specifications to include: Core construction material ("solid 3/4-inch birch or equivalent structural hardwood — particleboard, MDF, and laminate-over-substrate constructions are not acceptable"). Door construction and ventilation requirement (minimum open face percentage for ventilated doors). Hardware specifications (hinge gauge, lock type, nameplate style). Finish requirements (stain or paint, specific color references if applicable, top coat specification).

Dimensional specifications: Width, height, depth for each locker configuration. Internal configuration: shelf positions, hanging rod height, base clearance. ADA-accessible locker configurations and quantities.

### Section 4: Design and Customisation Requirements

Specify the customisation elements required: team colors (provide Pantone references or paint codes), logo integration (provide vector artwork files), player nameplate format (engraved, routed, insert style), and any other program-specific design elements.

Note that design services should be included at no additional charge. Any vendor who charges separately for a design consultation for a project of this scale should be noted in your evaluation.

### Section 5: Project Timeline

Specify your target installation date and ask vendors to provide a project schedule that works backward from that date. Key milestones to request: design approval date, manufacturing start date, delivery date, installation start and completion dates.

This section also reveals vendor capacity. A vendor who cannot commit to your timeline doesn't have available production capacity — which tells you something about their market position and ability to serve your project.

### Section 6: Pricing Format

Require vendors to provide itemized pricing in a consistent format: per-locker cost by configuration, design fees (if any), delivery cost, installation cost, and total project cost. Requiring the same pricing format from every vendor allows direct comparison.

Also require vendors to specify their pricing validity period — how long the quoted price is valid. Locker room bids with no stated validity period can be re-priced before contract execution.

### Section 7: Warranty Terms

Require vendors to provide their complete warranty document as an exhibit to their proposal. Summary warranty language ("lifetime warranty") in the proposal body is not sufficient. You need the actual warranty terms: duration, coverage scope, exclusions, claim process, and what voids coverage.

This section is where significant quality differences between vendors become visible. A vendor whose warranty document is a paragraph in the proposal body is telling you the warranty isn't a serious commitment. A vendor who provides a multi-page warranty document with specific coverage terms has thought through what they're promising.

### Section 8: Evaluation Criteria

Tell vendors exactly how proposals will be scored. A transparent scoring matrix serves two purposes: it creates a fair evaluation framework, and it signals to vendors what you value — which often improves the quality of responses.

A recommended weighting for locker room projects: technical specifications compliance (25%), total cost of ownership including warranty value (25%), vendor qualifications and references (20%), project timeline feasibility (15%), design quality and customisation capability (15%).

## Writing Specifications That Produce Comparable Bids

The technical specifications section is where most RFPs fail. Underspecification allows low-quality vendors to compete; over-specification (specifying a brand) may violate procurement rules. The goal is performance specifications — standards that define what the product must do and what it must be made of, without naming a specific vendor.

### The Material Specification

This single line prevents the most common low-quality bid from being technically compliant: "Core construction shall be solid 3/4-inch birch or equivalent structural hardwood. Particleboard, medium-density fiberboard (MDF), and laminate-over-substrate composite constructions are not acceptable and will be disqualified."

If your procurement rules require you to justify performance specifications, the justification is simple: athletic locker rooms are high-humidity environments with significant physical demand on construction materials. Particleboard and MDF are structurally compromised by moisture exposure and cannot provide the service life required for a capital facility investment. Solid hardwood construction is the minimum structural standard for this application.

### Ventilation Specifications

Specify minimum ventilation: "Locker doors shall provide a minimum open face ventilation area of 35% of total door face area. Solid, non-ventilated doors are not acceptable." This single specification ensures every bid includes meaningful ventilation without specifying a particular door style.

### ADA Specifications

Reference [ADA.gov standards](https://www.ada.gov) directly: "At minimum, [X% of total locker count, consistent with ADA requirements] lockers shall be configured for accessible use, meeting the reach range, clear floor space, and operable hardware requirements of the Americans with Disabilities Act and applicable state accessibility codes."

## Evaluation Criteria and the Scoring Matrix

A weighted scoring matrix protects you in two ways: it produces a defensible evaluation record, and it prevents a single criterion (typically price) from overriding other important factors.

For locker room projects, a workable matrix looks like this. Technical specifications compliance: 25 points — does the proposal meet every material, dimensional, and ventilation requirement specified? Price and total cost of ownership: 25 points — initial price plus maintenance cost estimate plus warranty replacement value. Vendor qualifications and references: 20 points — years in business, comparable project experience, quality of references provided. Timeline feasibility: 15 points — can the vendor meet the required installation date with documentation of their production capacity? Design quality: 15 points — clarity and professionalism of design renderings, customisation capability, evidence of previous comparable work.

Score each proposal against every criterion before comparing proposals. The temptation is to start with price and work backward. Resist it — the purpose of the matrix is to ensure comprehensive evaluation.

## Red Flags in Vendor Responses

The quality of a vendor's proposal response tells you as much as the proposal content. Watch for these warning signs.

**Vague warranty language.** A proposal that says "lifetime warranty" without providing the warranty document is telling you the warranty isn't a serious commitment. Press for the document.

**No design consultation included.** Quality locker manufacturers include a free design consultation as a standard part of the proposal process. A vendor charging separately for design work for a project of this scale is padding margin at your expense.

**No reference installation offer.** Any vendor unwilling to provide references you can actually visit — not just phone numbers — has something to hide about their installation quality.

**Substitution language.** Proposals that include language like "or equal" or "materials equivalent to specification at manufacturer's discretion" are reserving the right to deliver something other than what was specified. Reject this language.

**Implausibly short lead times.** If your RFP specifies custom solid wood lockers and a vendor promises 4-week delivery, they are not building custom lockers. They are pulling from commodity inventory or representing their capacity inaccurately.

## Timing: When to Issue the RFP

The critical path for a locker room project looks like this. Issue the RFP 20 weeks before your target installation completion date. Allow 2–3 weeks for the RFP question and clarification period. Allow 1 week for proposal submission. Allow 2 weeks for evaluation and vendor selection. Then allow 10–16 weeks for manufacturing and installation.

Programs targeting a summer installation (ideal for most academic programs) need to issue the RFP no later than February. Programs targeting a fall installation need to issue by May.

The most common mistake: assuming the 10–16 week manufacturing window can be compressed. It cannot. Custom solid wood lockers are built to order. There is no inventory to pull from. A program that selects a vendor in June and expects August installation is not getting summer lead times — they're getting the next available production slot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is an RFP required for a locker room purchase?

Most public school districts and state universities require a formal RFP process for purchases above a defined dollar threshold — commonly $25,000–$50,000 depending on the state. Private schools and independent colleges typically have more flexibility, but many still use an RFP process for large facility purchases to ensure competitive pricing and documented due diligence. When in doubt, check with your purchasing office or school board before proceeding with a direct purchase. A purchase made outside required procurement channels can be voided and create audit findings.

### What is the most common mistake athletic directors make when writing a locker room RFP?

Underspecifying the product. A vague spec — "wood lockers, 24 inches wide" — allows vendors to bid any wood product, including particleboard with a wood veneer. The result is bids that look comparable but represent radically different products. Specify material explicitly: "solid 3/4-inch birch, no particleboard, no MDF, no laminate-over-substrate construction." That single line eliminates a category of bids that would otherwise create post-award problems and early replacement cycles.

### How should I score bids that are not all on the same price basis?

Use a weighted scoring matrix that accounts for total cost of ownership, not just line-item price. Allocate roughly 40% of scoring weight to price and TCO, 20% to warranty terms and quality, 20% to vendor qualifications and references, 10% to timeline, and 10% to design quality. A vendor proposing solid wood at $449/locker with a lifetime guarantee often scores higher in a TCO analysis than a vendor proposing particleboard at $299/locker with a 5-year limited warranty — especially over a 20-year facility planning horizon.

### How long before my target installation should I issue the RFP?

Issue the RFP at least 20 weeks before your target installation date. Allow 2–3 weeks for the RFP process (questions, clarifications, submission). Allow 2 weeks for evaluation and vendor selection. Then allow 10–16 weeks for manufacturing and installation. Programs targeting a summer installation should issue the RFP no later than February. Programs targeting fall should issue by May. The manufacturing window is not compressible — plan around it.

### Can I specify solid wood in an RFP — or will that limit competition?

You can and should specify the material standard you require. Specifying "solid wood construction" or "solid 3/4-inch birch or equivalent structural hardwood" is a legitimate performance specification — it defines the durability and longevity standard your program requires, not a specific brand. Procurement guidelines allow performance specifications that set a minimum quality standard. The justification is straightforward: athletic locker rooms are high-humidity environments, and particleboard is structurally inappropriate for the application.

### What warranty language should I require in a locker room RFP?

Require vendors to provide their complete warranty document as an exhibit to their proposal. "Lifetime warranty" in the body text is marketing language, not a warranty commitment. The document should specify: what is covered, what voids coverage, the claim process, and the remedy (repair vs. replacement vs. credit). Compare the documents carefully — the quality gap between a real lifetime guarantee and a limited 5-year warranty becomes visible in the details.
