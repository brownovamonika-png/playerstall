## Hockey Locker Rooms: Michigan's Unique Requirements

### Size: Hockey Demands More Space Than Any Other Sport

If you've ever watched a hockey player fully gear up, you understand the storage challenge. Full pad set, skates, helmet with full cage, gloves, pants, jersey, practice gear, and a bag that holds it all—that's a volume that simply doesn't fit in a standard 18"–24" locker.

Michigan hockey programs need minimum 30" wide lockers. Programs serious about player experience and recruiting go to 36". The extra 6 inches isn't luxury—it's the difference between equipment that fits properly and gear that gets stuffed, damages itself, and frustrates players before they even hit the ice.

Height at 76" standard gives you overhead storage for helmets and the vertical space for hanging jerseys without cramming. Depth at 24" handles the bulk of pads and skate bags. Our design team specs each Michigan hockey installation individually because roster positions matter: goaltenders need 36"+ to accommodate full goalie kits, and those lockers need to be planned into the layout from the start.

### Stick Storage: This Gets Overlooked Until It Becomes a Problem

Every hockey player carries multiple sticks—typically 3–5 at the varsity level, sometimes more. Those sticks can't go in the locker with everything else; they need dedicated vertical storage that keeps them accessible without damage.

We integrate stick rack systems directly into locker room designs, positioned so players can grab and replace sticks without crossing traffic lanes. Wall-mounted stick racks adjacent to locker bays, numbered to correspond with player lockers, are the standard we recommend. A locker room designed without a stick storage plan ends up with sticks leaning against walls, falling in aisles, and getting damaged. That's avoidable.

### Ventilation: Wet Gear Needs to Dry

Hockey gear is wet after every skate. Skate boots in particular need significant air circulation to dry properly between sessions, and gear that doesn't dry develops odor and breaks down faster. Standard closed locker doors trap moisture against equipment. That's wrong for hockey.

Our hockey installations include ventilated door panels with deliberate airflow gaps, and we recommend that Michigan programs pair locker ventilation with room-level exhaust fans positioned to draw air across the locker bays. The combination keeps gear drier, extends equipment life, and keeps the locker room smelling professional for recruits and visiting parents.

## Michigan's Climate and Why Wood Wins

Let's talk about what Michigan winters do to metal.

A metal locker in a Michigan hockey rink sees salt-soaked boots tracking in every day from October through March. It sees wet jerseys and pads hanging in a space where humidity runs 60–80% after practice. It sees the freeze-thaw cycle that stresses every joint and finish coating. Metal locker paint chips at edges, hardware loosens at expansion joints, and corrosion starts at the base and works upward. Michigan programs that installed metal lockers in the early 2000s are dealing with structural failure right now.

Solid 3/4" birch handles this differently. Wood expands and contracts with humidity changes in a way that maintains structural integrity rather than stressing joints to failure. Our sealed finishes protect the surface from moisture penetration while the built-in ventilation gaps prevent the moisture accumulation that causes interior damage. The analogy that fits: a properly maintained wood deck handles Michigan winters for 20 years; a painted metal deck needs refinishing within five.

Michigan programs that ask us about metal alternatives rarely stick with that conversation for long. The climate argument is too clear. Over a 20-year period, wood lockers in Michigan are significantly cheaper than the combined cost of two or three metal installations.

## Funding Locker Room Upgrades in Michigan

Michigan districts have several funding paths for athletic facility improvements. Understanding which one fits your project size and timeline matters before you go to your administration.

**Sinking funds** are Michigan's most useful tool for locker room projects. A sinking fund millage allows districts to accumulate capital over time specifically for facility maintenance and improvement—and unlike general bond funding, sinking funds don't require a supermajority approval in most circumstances. If your district has an active sinking fund, a locker room renovation is a textbook appropriate use. The argument to your administration is straightforward: this is exactly what the fund was designed for.

**Bond proposals** fund larger renovation packages. Michigan districts regularly put athletic facility bonds on the ballot as part of broader capital improvement proposals. If your district is already planning a bond measure, lobbying to include the locker room renovation is far more efficient than a standalone campaign. Frame it as infrastructure—not athletics spending.

**Booster clubs and athletic foundations** handle the gap between what public funding covers and what a program actually wants. Michigan has strong booster cultures, particularly in hockey and football communities. A naming rights package attached to the locker room renovation—"The [Family] Hockey Locker Room"—can generate $20,000–$60,000 in contributions, depending on your program's profile and donor base.
