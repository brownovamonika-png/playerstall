## Girls Wrestling Lockers: Specifying a Room for a New Program

Girls wrestling has been one of the fastest-growing high school sports in the
United States for several years, and schools are adding programs faster than
they are adding the facilities to support them.

The pattern is familiar. A school sanctions girls wrestling, a coach is
appointed, athletes turn out, and then somebody has to answer the question of
where they change and where their gear lives. Frequently the answer, in the
first season, is a repurposed storage room, a corner of the boys' facility, or
nothing at all.

This guide is for athletic directors solving that properly, usually on a budget
that was not planned for it.

## The separate room is not optional

Start with the part that is not a design preference.

A girls wrestling program needs its own changing and storage space. Sharing the
boys' wrestling room is not workable, and splitting it by time, boys at one
hour, girls at another, fails as soon as both programs need the room on a
competition day, which is most of them.

There is also a **Title IX** dimension. Title IX requires comparable treatment
of male and female athletic programs, and locker rooms are explicitly among the
facilities considered in that assessment. A school running an established boys'
wrestling room and a girls' team changing in a converted closet has a difference
that is easy to see and difficult to justify.

The standard is **comparable, not identical**. The girls' room does not need to
be the same dimensions. It does need to be the same quality of provision, same
model of locker, same finish, same fit-out. Specifying the same line for both
rooms is the simplest way to make that true, and the simplest thing to defend
to a board.

## Wrestling gear is small, which is the budget advantage

Here is the genuinely good news for a new program.

Wrestling has one of the smallest gear footprints in school athletics:

- A singlet or fight shorts and top
- Headgear
- Wrestling shoes
- Knee pads, sometimes
- A change of clothes
- A bag

That is it. No pads, no helmet, no sticks, no bats, no bulky outerwear
requirement beyond what any student carries in winter.

**Eighteen to twenty inches is genuinely sufficient.** Not a compromise, the
correct specification. Programs that size a wrestling room at 30 inches per
position are paying for space the sport does not use.

For a new girls program on a budget that was found rather than planned, this
matters. A full roster can usually be outfitted properly at a sensible width,
rather than half the roster being covered at a generous one. Our minimum order
is 8 lockers, which is low enough that a first-year team of ten or twelve can be
done completely in its first season.

## Hygiene is a specification issue, not a cleaning issue

Wrestling carries a documented skin-infection risk, it is a close-contact
sport, skin to skin and skin to mat, and the gear is damp. Managing that is
partly a hygiene protocol and partly a facilities decision, and the facilities
half is decided when you specify the room.

What to specify:

**A sealed finish that takes repeated disinfecting.** Wrestling rooms get wiped
down far more often than other athletic spaces. The surface has to survive that
without degrading, absorbing or going cloudy.

**A raised base.** So the floor under the lockers can actually be cleaned. A
locker sitting flat on the floor creates a strip nobody can reach, in a room
where that matters more than most.

**Vented fronts and airflow.** Damp gear in a sealed box stays damp. Damp gear
in a wrestling room is specifically the thing you are trying to avoid. Our
3-slot vented front exists for this.

**No surfaces that cannot be wiped.** Anything soft or porous in a wrestling
room becomes a cleaning problem.

**Hooks so gear hangs.** Singlets and headgear drying on a hook dry faster than
the same items folded on a shelf.

None of these are expensive decisions. They are all specification decisions made
once, and they determine how much work the room is to keep safe for the next
fifteen years.

## Practical notes for a first-year program

A few things that come up specifically with new girls programs.

**Roster growth.** First-year rosters are often small and grow quickly. Specify
the model and finish you would want at full size, and leave wall space. Adding
eight matching lockers in year two is straightforward; matching a discontinued
model is not.

**Shared seasons.** Girls wrestling runs in winter, overlapping with basketball
and indoor track. If the room is shared, use removable name plates at $10 and
specify for the bulkier sport.

**Identity.** A new program benefits more than an established one from looking
like a real program. A team logo at $75 per locker is one of the cheapest
things in the build and among the most visible, for athletes deciding whether
to turn out next year, and for parents deciding whether this is a serious
offering.

**Security for commuting athletes.** A keypad lock box at $80 secures phone,
wallet and keys with no keys for the program to issue or collect.

## If you are already running a boys' room

Two situations come up.

**You are adding girls wrestling to a school with an established boys' room.**
Specify the girls' room in the same line and finish. If the boys' room is dated
metal and the girls' room would be new wood, you have created the comparison in
reverse, which is better than the usual direction, but worth thinking about as
a plan to bring both to the same standard.

**You are renovating both at once.** This is the best case, and it is cheaper
per locker than two separate orders. Specify together, order together, and the
comparability question answers itself.

## Conclusion

Schools are adding girls wrestling faster than they are adding rooms for it, and
the first season is usually improvised. It does not have to be, because
wrestling is one of the least demanding sports to specify for: small gear
footprint, modest widths, and a short list of decisions that make the room
cleanable and keep gear dry.

Give the program its own space. Specify it in the same line as the boys' room, 
comparable is the standard, and it is the easy thing to defend. Size honestly at
18 to 20 inches rather than paying for width the sport does not use. Specify a
sealed finish, a raised base, vented fronts and hooks, because in wrestling
those are health decisions rather than comfort ones. And leave wall space,
because the roster will grow.
