## Softball Lockers: The Complete Guide for Programs in 2027

**Softball is one of the largest girls' sports in the United States and one of the least considered when locker rooms get built. The gear is bulkier than people assume, the sport is played on dirt, the season runs through cold spring weather and into summer heat, and the room very often sits in whatever space was left after baseball was taken care of. This guide covers what softball storage actually requires — bats, helmets with faceguards, catcher's gear, two kinds of cleats, and the clay that follows all of it indoors — along with sizing, material, cost, and the facility-equity question that is increasingly part of the conversation.**

The [NFHS 2024-25 participation survey](https://nfhs.org/stories/participation-in-high-school-sports-hits-record-high-with-sizable-increase-in-2024-25) counted 331,306 girls playing fast pitch softball, making it the fifth most popular girls program in American high schools out of a record 8,266,244 total participants. Add college rosters across NCAA, NAIA, and NJCAA and you have a very large athlete population — served, in a great many schools, by lockers that were never specified with the sport in mind.

We have built wood athletic lockers for over 35 years. Here is what we have learned about getting a softball room right.

## Key Takeaways

- Softball gear is bulkier than the roster count suggests — plan 20 to 24 inches per athlete, and more for catchers.
- Bats belong in a shared vertical rack, not inside individual lockers. A 34-inch bat ruins the interior layout of a locker it is forced into.
- Dirt and infield clay are the defining design problem. Grated base shelves, base clearance, and a sealed wipe-clean finish solve most of it.
- Catchers carry roughly double the equipment of any other position and should be specified separately rather than averaged in.
- Locker rooms are explicitly within the scope of Title IX facility comparisons. Upgrading baseball without upgrading softball is a pattern worth avoiding.

## What Softball Gear Actually Requires

Walk a softball dugout at the end of practice and count what each athlete carries. A glove. A helmet with an attached faceguard. A bat, often two. Metal cleats and turf shoes. Sliding shorts and knee savers. A batting glove pair. A warm-up jacket for the first six weeks of the season. A water bottle. For catchers, add a chest protector, shin guards, and a separate catcher's helmet.

That is more volume than a basketball or wrestling athlete and it is shaped awkwardly — long, rigid, and non-folding. The common mistake is to treat softball as a low-equipment sport because the athletes are not wearing football pads. The gear does not stack, and that is what drives the locker requirement.

Three items set the design constraints, and each one deserves its own treatment: the bat, the helmet, and the glove.

### The Helmet With a Faceguard

A softball batting helmet with a faceguard does not sit flat and cannot be stacked. It needs an open shelf with clear height above it, ideally the top shelf of the locker where the cage is not pressed against anything. Programs that store helmets in a bag or stuffed under a shelf end up with bent faceguards, which is both a cost and a safety issue.

### The Glove

A glove must keep its shape. Crushed under a helmet or wedged beside a bat, the pocket collapses and the glove needs breaking in again. The right place is an open shelf at mid height where nothing is stacked on it. Some athletes keep a ball in the pocket with a band around it — that works, and it needs the clearance to sit undisturbed.

Both of those requirements point the same direction: softball lockers need **open shelving at multiple heights**, not one deep cavity. A single large opening means everything ends up in a pile, and a pile is how gloves get crushed and faceguards get bent.

## Bats: Why They Belong on a Wall

A fast pitch bat runs 33 or 34 inches. Stand one inside a 72-inch locker and it either occupies the full lower half, blocking the shelf above, or it leans diagonally against whatever is hanging. Two bats per athlete makes it worse. Lay them horizontally and they roll off the shelf.

The solution is a shared vertical bat rack along one wall of the room, holding the entire roster's bats upright and visible. This gives three advantages at once. The full locker interior is freed for everything else. Bats stop getting knocked around inside lockers, which matters for composite bats that can be damaged by impact. And the coaching staff can see the whole bat inventory at a glance, which is genuinely useful when equipment is school-owned and has to be accounted for.

This is the same reasoning behind the stick racks we build for hockey programs — a long rigid implement is better stored communally and vertically than individually and awkwardly. We have written about [hockey stick racks](/blog/hockey-stick-racks) and the logic transfers directly.

If bats must live in individual lockers — some programs require it for security or because athletes own their own — specify a taller locker. At 84 inches there is room for a bat in a dedicated vertical channel with usable shelving above it. At 72 inches there is not.

## The Catcher Problem

Catchers are the single most under-specified position in softball locker room design.

A catcher carries a chest protector, shin guards, a catcher's helmet or mask, and a catcher's mitt, on top of everything every other player has. In practical terms that is close to double the equipment volume, and almost all of it is bulky, rigid, and heavily sweat-soaked.

Averaging that across the roster does not work. Give every athlete an identical 20-inch locker and your two or three catchers will overflow while half the room runs half empty.

The fix is simple and cheap at specification time: **designate two or three wider lockers for catchers.** Twenty-four inches minimum, ideally with an extra open shelf and an additional hook. Position them where the gear can be laid out rather than crammed. If you are ordering 20 lockers, ordering 17 at one width and 3 at another costs very little extra and solves the problem permanently.

Shin guards and chest protectors also need to hang rather than sit. They hold moisture in their padding, and a chest protector folded on a shelf after practice is still damp the next day. Hooks sized for the straps, with air around the gear, are what dries them.

## Dirt, Clay, and the Base of the Locker

This is the thing that makes softball different from every indoor sport, and it is almost never addressed in locker room design.

Softball is played on dirt and infield clay. Every athlete walks off the field with it packed into metal cleats and dusted across everything in their bag. It then comes into the locker room and settles — into the base of the locker, under the locker, and onto whatever shelf the cleats land on. Clay in particular is fine, staining, and gets into surfaces.

Three specifications handle it:

**A grated or slatted base shelf.** Cleats sit on the grating; dirt falls through to a surface that can be swept. A solid shelf collects a layer of dirt that then transfers to anything else placed there — including, routinely, the glove.

**Base clearance under the locker.** Four inches between the locker bottom and the floor means dirt does not pack into an inaccessible gap, and the floor can actually be cleaned. A locker sitting flush to the floor in a dirt sport builds up a sediment line that never comes out.

**A sealed finish that takes repeated damp cleaning.** A softball room needs wiping down regularly, not seasonally. The finish has to tolerate that without absorbing water or breaking down. Sealed birch does; unsealed wood and compromised powder coat do not.

Keeping cleats separated from everything else is most of the battle. A two-tier base shelf — metal cleats below, turf shoes above — is a small addition that keeps the dirtiest item in the room in one defined place.

## Weather: Cold April, Hot June

Softball is an outdoor spring sport in most of the country, which means the season starts in genuinely cold weather and finishes in heat. The locker has to serve both ends.

Early season means warm-up jackets, long sleeves, sweatpants, and sometimes gloves and beanies — bulky layers that need hanging space and that are often damp from rain or wet grass. Late season means sweat volume, sun sleeves, and multiple shirts per session.

The practical implication is that softball needs **more hanging space than an indoor sport of comparable roster size**, and it needs that hanging space ventilated. A damp jacket hung in a sealed locker on a cold April evening is still damp at Friday's game. Vented fronts matter here for the same reason they matter in wrestling, even though the moisture arrives differently.

Depth matters too. A jacket pressed flat against the back panel of an 18-inch-deep locker dries on one side. Twenty-two to twenty-three inches of depth lets layers hang with air on both sides.

## Sizing and Configuration

Pulling the above together, here is what we specify for a typical softball program.

| Element | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Width | 20–24″ for field players; 24″+ for catchers | Gear is bulky and non-folding |
| Height | 76–84″ | 84″ if bats are stored in-locker |
| Depth | 22–23″ | Layered spring clothing needs air around it |
| Front | Vented | Damp outerwear and sweat-soaked padding |
| Base shelf | Grated, ideally two-tier | Cleats, dirt, clay |
| Base clearance | 4″ | Floor cleaning, no packed sediment |
| Shelving | Open, multiple heights | Helmet clear above, glove uncrushed |
| Hooks | 4+, one heavy-duty | Jacket, bag, catcher's gear straps |
| Bats | Shared vertical wall rack | A 34″ bat wrecks a locker interior |
| Name plates | Framed, removable | Annual roster turnover |

For roster count, order for the roster plus about 15 percent. Softball programs carry JV and varsity, call athletes up mid-season, and add players. Matching a custom finish and trim two years later is the hard part, so build the spare capacity in at the start.

## Softball and Baseball: Sharing, and Why It Usually Fails

Schools frequently propose that softball and baseball share a locker room, on the reasoning that they are similar sports with similar gear.

The problem is the calendar. In most states the softball and baseball seasons run almost entirely concurrently. Sharing a locker across two sports works when the seasons alternate — the way wrestling and football often can — but two spring sports sharing lockers means two athletes assigned to one locker during the same months. That is not storage; that is a conflict.

Where facility constraints genuinely force sharing, specify for it honestly: wider lockers than either sport needs alone, removable name plates so assignment is flexible, and identical ventilation throughout. Our [multi-sport locker room design guide](/blog/multi-sport-locker-room-design) covers the general approach.

But be careful about which program ends up with what. In shared arrangements the softball program very often receives the smaller share, the older lockers, or the room further from the field. That is where this becomes more than a design question.

## Title IX and Facility Equity

Title IX requires that schools treat men's and women's athletic programs comparably, and locker rooms and practice facilities are explicitly among the facilities considered in that assessment. It is not only about scholarship dollars or roster spots.

Softball facilities being materially worse than baseball facilities at the same institution is a well-documented pattern in American school athletics — smaller rooms, older fixtures, less storage, sometimes no dedicated room at all while the baseball program has one. Programs and athletes have raised formal concerns about exactly these disparities, and facilities are usually among the first items examined when they do.

The practical guidance for athletic directors is straightforward:

**Upgrade the pair together.** If baseball is getting new lockers, specify softball in the same project. One production run costs less per locker than two, the two rooms match, and the equity question never arises. This is the same logic that led Iowa Western to outfit its men's and women's wrestling programs in [a single 102-locker order](/blog/iowa-western-wrestling-locker-room-case-study).

**Document what each program has.** Locker count, width, room size, storage, and age. If you cannot compare them on paper, you cannot demonstrate comparability.

**Do not treat "the softball team didn't ask" as an answer.** The programs that ask loudest tend to be the ones already well resourced.

None of this requires a large budget. It requires specifying both programs at once instead of sequentially. For more on designing with female athletes specifically in mind, see our guide to [women's sports lockers](/blog/womens-sports-lockers-female-athletes).

## Material: Wood Against Metal in a Dirt Sport

Softball is a strong case for wood over metal, for reasons specific to the sport.

**Bats and metal doors do not mix.** A dropped or swung bat dents a metal locker door, and a dented door often stops closing properly. Wood takes the same impact with a mark rather than a deformation. In a room where 34-inch aluminium and composite bats are carried past lockers twice a day, this is not hypothetical.

**Dirt plus chipped powder coat equals rust.** Metal lockers chip at the edges and corners first. Dirt and moisture then sit in the chip, and that is where corrosion starts. In a sport that brings damp clay indoors daily, chips become rust faster than in an indoor sport.

**Cleaning frequency.** A softball room needs wiping down often. Sealed birch tolerates repeated damp cleaning and disinfectant. Thin powder coat degrades under the same routine.

**Noise.** Metal lockers in a room full of bats and cleats are loud. Wood is noticeably quieter, which matters more than people expect in a space where a team meets before games.

## Conclusion

Softball storage is not baseball storage with a different sign on the door. The bats are a different length, the helmets have faceguards that will not stack, the catcher carries double, the season starts cold and ends hot, and every single day the sport brings infield clay indoors. A room specified with those five facts in mind works. A room specified as "the same as the boys but smaller" does not, and increasingly invites a question the school would rather not answer.

The good news is that none of it is expensive to get right. Grated base shelves, a wall bat rack, three wider lockers for catchers, vented fronts, and ordering alongside baseball instead of two years after it — those are specification decisions, not budget decisions.
